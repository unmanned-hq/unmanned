## Day 2, for grant writers

A proposal for a grant you never had a chance of winning takes just as long to write as one you win.

Before drafting, answer three questions honestly:

1. Does your work *directly* match the funder's stated priorities?
2. Do you meet every hard eligibility criterion? If any is unconfirmed, stop and check.
3. Have you delivered this kind of work at this scale?

If all three answers are strong, write it. If any is weak, let it go, and write down why in one sentence.

The full routine, with a worked example, is in the article linked in the comments. I also made
it into a free Claude skill.

## Day 3, a failure I'm publishing

I caught one of my own products making things up.

My free skill for academic reviewer response letters passed its tests 33 out of 33. Then a
review found that in one test it had invented methods details the author never gave it: a
matching procedure and a supplementary table that didn't exist. I can't think of a worse failure
for a letter that goes to a journal.

The tests rewarded specifics, so the made-up ones passed.

It's now instructed to turn anything the author hasn't told it into a visible [placeholder]. I
re-ran every test with fresh agents and a separate grader, and a second review caught one more
failure the grader had missed. The new score is 39 of 40. In the failed case it wrote a change as
already made, and that failure is published alongside the results.

I'd rather publish 39 of 40 than keep a 33 of 33 I know is wrong.

## Day 4, for researchers

A response to reviewers should make it easy to see what happened to every comment. Here's how I'd
build one:

- Decide accept, partial or push back *before* drafting.
- Number everything, and split bundled comments.
- Quote each comment, acknowledge it, then say what you changed and where.
- Back up any pushback with evidence.
- Never write a change you didn't make.

The full guide, with a template, is in the comments.

## Day 5, for newsletter operators

If an issue is hard to write, the problem is usually upstream of the writing. You have a topic,
not a point.

"Sponsorship pricing" is a topic.
"Small newsletters undercharge because they price on CPM instead of what their list delivers"
is a point.

The plan in my guide fits on one page. Sharpen the claim, write three hooks, set three sections
with word budgets, pick one CTA and draft three subject lines. Then write.
