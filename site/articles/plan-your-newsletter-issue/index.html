Plan a newsletter issue on one page before you write it

When an issue is hard to write, the problem is usually upstream of the writing. You have a topic but not a point. "AI in the workplace" is a topic. "Most teams adopting AI tools are measuring the wrong thing" is a point. You can write the second one. The first one produces a blank page.

Below is a one-page plan for getting from a topic to a point before you draft anything.

1. Sharpen the topic into a claim someone could disagree with

Write your topic down, then keep narrowing until a reasonable reader could argue with it.

  • Topic: sponsorship pricing
  • Narrower: most small newsletters price sponsorships badly
  • Claim: *small newsletters price sponsorships badly because they borrow the per-thousand (CPM)

maths built for big lists, when they should charge a flat fee based on who their readers are*

If nobody could disagree with it, it isn't a claim yet. If you can't support it, it's the wrong claim, so narrow in a different direction.

2. Write three hooks, one of each type

The headline and opening line are there to keep the right reader reading. Write three:

  • A direct one that says the point. Small newsletters shouldn't price sponsorships by CPM.
  • A curiosity hook that opens a gap the issue closes. *The pricing habit that makes a small

newsletter look cheaper than it is.*

  • A contrarian one that names the belief you're challenging. *Your list isn't too small to charge

properly. Your pricing formula is the problem.*

Pick whichever suits your list. Another one will often make a good subject line.

3. Set three sections and a word budget

Three sections is enough for most issues:

  1. What's going wrong, and who it's happening to.
  2. Your claim, with the reasoning or example that supports it.
  3. The concrete change a reader can make this week.

Give each section a rough word count before you write. Otherwise section one tends to eat the issue.

4. Choose exactly one call to action

Ask readers to reply, share, buy and follow, and most will do none of them. If you don't know what your list responds to yet, ask for a reply: "how did you arrive at your current sponsorship price?" The replies also tell you what to write next.

5. Draft three subject lines

Use the same three types as the hooks: direct, curiosity and contrarian. Test them over a few issues and you'll learn what your list opens.

The finished plan

This is what the free skill returned when I gave it the topic from step 1. The request was: "Plan my next issue. Topic: most small newsletters price sponsorships badly. My newsletter is for people who run newsletters." The output is pasted as it came back. The claim and the hooks in the steps above are taken from it.

Assumption: Your readers are solo or small-team newsletter operators who sell, or are about to sell, their own sponsorships. You gave the audience as "people who run newsletters"; I narrowed it to those who handle sponsorships themselves.

Sharpened topic

  • Starting point: "Most small newsletters price sponsorships badly."
  • Sharpened: Small newsletters price sponsorships badly because they borrow the per-thousand (CPM) maths built for big lists, when they should charge a flat fee based on who their readers are.

This is my proposed reading of "badly", not something you told me. If you meant something else (too low, no rate card, discounting too quickly), tell me and I'll re-plan around that.

Hook/headline options

  • Direct: Small newsletters shouldn't price sponsorships by CPM
  • Curiosity: The pricing habit that makes a small newsletter look cheaper than it is
  • Contrarian: Your list isn't too small to charge properly. Your pricing formula is the problem.

Issue structure

SectionDescriptionWords
1. The borrowed formulaHow small operators usually arrive at a price, and why list size multiplied by a going rate undersells a small list. Anchor it with [a real rate or pricing example you have seen or used].150
2. What a sponsor is actually buyingThe case for pricing on audience fit and the sponsor's outcome instead of raw reach. Support it with [your evidence: a sponsor conversation, a result, or a comparison you can stand behind].350
3. Re-pricing your next slotA short, practical way for the reader to set a flat fee and present it to a sponsor, using [the steps or rule of thumb you actually recommend].200

Total: 700 words.

CTA

Reply with your take: how did you arrive at your current sponsorship price?

I don't know your subscriber count, so this is the default. If you have 500 or more, tell me and I'll swap in a purchase, hire or referral ask.

Subject lines

  • Direct: Why CPM pricing fails small newsletters
  • Curiosity gap: You priced your sponsorship slot by list size, and...
  • Contrarian: A small list is not a reason to charge less

Before you write

The bracketed placeholders need your own material. I have not supplied any rates, results or statistics, because you didn't give me any.

The square brackets are where your own rates, results and examples go. The skill leaves them empty because it only works with what you told it.
