When to push back on a reviewer

Every revision has a comment that makes you want to start typing a rebuttal straight away. Sometimes the rebuttal is right. Often, though, the reviewer has spotted a real problem and asked for the wrong fix, and the useful answer sits somewhere between giving in and digging in.

Here's a way to make that call one comment at a time, before you write any of the letter.

Separate the concern from the request

Most reviewer comments contain two things: a concern ("the sample may not represent the wider population") and a requested fix ("recruit from a second site"). You can agree with one and not the other. Writing them down separately is most of the work.

Then ask four questions about each comment:

  1. Is the concern valid? Would a careful reader of the paper share it?
  2. Is the requested fix proportionate? What would it cost in time, data or money?
  3. Is it within the scope of this paper, or is it a different study?
  4. If you disagree, can you point to evidence: your design, your data, published work or clear reasoning?

Turn the answers into a decision

Concern valid?Fix reasonable?Decision
YesYesAccept
YesNo, too costly or out of scopePartial, with a narrower change and a stated limitation
No, they misread the paperNot relevantAccept as a clarification
No, and you have evidenceNot relevantPush back
No, and you only have a feelingNot relevantThink again

The misreading row is the one people skip. If a reviewer misunderstood your method, other readers will too. Pushing back on a misreading wins the argument and leaves the confusing paragraph in the paper. Rewriting the paragraph is usually less work.

Irritation isn't evidence. If you can't state your reason in one sentence, you don't have a pushback yet.

Treat any comment the editor's letter singles out as required. If you still disagree, offer the most you can.

Each pushback asks a reviewer to concede a point, so save them for requests that would make the paper wrong or need a study you can't run. Turning a figure into a table isn't worth the fight.

How to word a pushback

A good pushback shows you understood the concern, gives your reason and offers something where it can. Fill in the brackets.

When the concern is fair but the fix is too big:

We agree that [concern]. Addressing it fully would require [what the fix needs], which is beyond the scope of this study. We have [narrower change] in [location] and now discuss this as a limitation in [location].

When you disagree about method:

We understand the concern that [restated concern]. We chose [approach] because [reason from your design, data or the literature]. To show that the result does not depend on this choice, we now report [sensitivity analysis or check] in [location].

When the reviewer misread:

We can see how the original wording suggested [what they understood]. In fact, [what you did]. We have rewritten [location] to make this explicit.

When reviewers contradict each other:

Reviewer 1 asked for [request], while Reviewer 2 suggested [opposite request]. We have [what you chose] because [reason], and we would welcome the editor's view.

Cut these on sight: "the reviewer is mistaken", "as clearly stated in the manuscript", and any sentence containing "obviously". They make the reviewer defend their pride instead of weighing your reason. And never prop up a pushback with a paper you haven't read or a result you don't have.

A hypothetical example

A reviewer writes: "The six-month follow-up is too short to assess durable effects. The authors should extend follow-up to two years."

Is the concern valid? Yes. Six months can't show whether an effect lasts. Is the fix reasonable? No. Two more years of follow-up is a new study. So the decision is Partial:

We agree that six months of follow-up cannot establish whether the effects persist. Extending follow-up to two years is not possible within this cohort. We have removed the word "durable" from the abstract and discussion, and we now name longer follow-up as a priority for future work in the Limitations section ([location]).

The reviewer didn't get two more years of data, but the overclaim they spotted is gone.
