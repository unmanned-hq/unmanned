How to test subject lines on a small list

You can split a 300-person list in half, send each half a different subject line and pick a winner an hour later. With a list that size, the winner is mostly luck. Here's why, and a slower method that tells you more.

Why a small split test misleads

Take that hypothetical list of 300. Each subject line goes to 150 people. Version A gets 60 opens (40%) and version B gets 68 (about 45%). That looks like a clear win for B.

It's eight people. Eight people who happened to check their email at the right moment, or who would have opened anything you sent that day. Run the same two lines again next week and the result could easily flip. A gap of that size between groups that small is well within what chance alone produces.

Open rates add their own noise. Some mail apps fetch images automatically, which registers as an open whether or not anyone read the email. Apple Mail's privacy protection is the best-known case. So the number you're comparing is partly people and partly software.

You can still learn what your readers respond to. You just learn it across issues, not within one.

Write three different kinds of subject line

For each issue, write at least three subject lines that work in different ways, not three rewordings of one idea. For a hypothetical issue about welcome emails:

  • A direct line that says what's inside: "What to put in your welcome email"
  • A curiosity line that asks the question the issue answers: "Is your welcome email doing anything?"
  • A contrarian line that challenges something readers assume: "Your welcome email is too long"

Pick the one you'd click if a friend sent it. Then look at it in your own phone's inbox, next to the preview text, because that's where it competes. If the first few words don't make sense on their own, rewrite it.

Set the preview text on purpose while you're there. Left empty, it usually shows whatever text comes first in the email, which might be a "view in browser" link.

Rotate styles across issues and keep a log

Instead of splitting each send, send each issue to everyone with one subject line, and rotate the style: direct one week, curiosity the next, contrarian after that. Keep a log. Two hypothetical rows:

After eight or ten issues, look for patterns rather than winners. Does one style land near the top more often than the others? Do certain topics pull replies whatever the subject line says? You're looking for something that shows up again and again, not one good week.

This is cruder than a real experiment, since every issue has a different topic and a different week. But it's honest about what a small list can tell you, and a pattern that holds across ten issues is worth more than one split test on 300 people.

Look at clicks and replies too

Opens are the noisiest number you have. Clicks and replies are smaller, but each one means a person did something. A line with a high open rate and almost no clicks may have promised something the issue didn't deliver. That's worth knowing even when the open rate looks good.

Ask your readers now and then

Once in a while, end an issue with one question: "What made you open this one?" Or list your three candidate subject lines and ask which they would have clicked. The answers won't be statistically meaningful either. They do come with reasons, which a split test never gives you.

When a split test starts to make sense

At some point your list is big enough for each half to give a stable answer. Where that point sits depends on how small a difference you want to detect, and small differences need much bigger groups. Before trusting any split result, put your numbers into a free significance calculator. Until the numbers hold up, the log is the better tool.
