How to structure a response to peer reviewers

A response letter should show the editor and reviewers that every concern was taken seriously and exactly what happened to each one. Good revisions can still get a bad reception if the letter makes reviewers go looking for them.

This is the structure I'd use to save them that work.

1. Decide before you write

Go through every comment and decide what you're doing about it before you draft a single response. There are only three honest options. You can accept the comment and make the change. You can accept the concern but make a narrower change than the reviewer asked for, which is a partial. Or you can respectfully disagree and explain why, which is pushback.

Drafting before deciding is how letters end up vague. "We have addressed this concern" usually means the author hadn't yet worked out whether they had.

2. Number everything, and split bundled comments

Give every distinct point an ID: R1.1, R1.2, R2.1. If one paragraph from Reviewer 2 contains three requests, it gets three IDs. Reviewers notice when the second half of their comment was silently dropped, and the numbering makes that impossible.

3. Quote, then respond

For each ID, quote the comment, then answer it. Every response does three things:

  1. It acknowledges the point in one sentence. That sentence should show you understood the comment, not flatter the reviewer.
  2. It says what you did, specifically. "We added a paragraph to Section 2.3 describing how controls were matched" beats "we clarified the methods."
  3. It says where: section, paragraph, line or figure. If the reviewer has to hunt, you've lost them.

4. Back up pushback with evidence

Disagreeing is fine, and reviewers are often wrong. Disagreeing loudly is what doesn't work. A pushback that works restates the reviewer's concern fairly, so they can see you heard it. Then it gives the reason, whether that's your design, the literature or the logic of the analysis. If you can, it also offers something, such as a sensitivity analysis or a sentence acknowledging the limitation.

Compare "The reviewer is mistaken; random-effects models handle heterogeneity" with "We agree that heterogeneity is high (I² = 78%). We used a random-effects model, which is designed for this situation, and we now report prediction intervals alongside the pooled estimate so readers can see the spread." The position is the same. Only the second one gets accepted.

(The numbers in that example are illustrative. Use your own.)

5. Never write a change you didn't make

This rule matters more than the others, and it's the easiest one to break when you're tired or using an AI tool. If the letter says you added a supplementary table, the table must exist. Reviewers check, and one invented change can cost you the trust every other response depends on.

If you haven't decided or haven't made a change yet, leave a visible placeholder like [describe the matching criteria you used] and fill it in before you submit. Don't fill the gap with something that merely sounds right.

6. Open short, close with a summary

Open with three sentences: thank the reviewers, say how many comments you addressed, and say the manuscript has been revised in response. Close with a bulleted list of the major changes, one line each. Editors often read the summary first.

A template

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for the careful reviews. We have addressed all [N] comments below; changes in the manuscript are marked in [colour / tracked changes].

Reviewer 1

Comment R1.1: "[quoted comment]"

Classification: Accept

Response: [One-sentence acknowledgement.] [What you changed.] [Where.]

Summary of major changes

  • [One line per change]
