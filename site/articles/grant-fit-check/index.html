A grant fit check to run before you write anything

A proposal for a grant you never had a real chance of winning takes as many days to write as one you win, and you learn less from it. The debrief, if you get one, arrives months later and rarely says "you were never a fit."

The day a promising RFP lands is when the pressure to start drafting is highest, so it's also the day a fit check is most likely to be skipped. Here's one you can do in a single sitting, before any drafting, with nothing but the RFP and an honest description of your organisation.

Start with what the funder wants, in its own words

Pull three to five priorities straight from the RFP. Quote them; don't paraphrase. Paraphrasing is where wishful thinking creeps in. "Expanding access to stable housing" quietly becomes "anything that helps people who might lose their housing," and suddenly your workforce programme looks like a fit.

If the RFP lists scoring criteria, start there. They tell you what reviewers will be told to reward.

Grade mission, eligibility and capacity

Each gets one of three grades, Strong, Partial or Weak, and one line saying why.

Mission alignment comes first. Does your work directly match what the funder said it cares about? Directly, not "could be framed as." One test I like: if a programme officer read your mission statement cold, would they see their priority in it?

Eligibility means every hard criterion: geography, organisation type, budget range, years operating, restrictions on prior funding. In practice it's binary. If any criterion is unconfirmed, eligibility is not Strong, and you should call the funder or check the FAQ before going further.

Capacity is about whether you have the track record and staff to deliver what this funder expects at this award size. A pilot workshop is not a track record for a three-year programme grant. Be specific about what you've actually delivered.

Turn the grades into a verdict

  • High if all three are Strong, or at most one is Partial.
  • Medium if one or two are Partial and none is Weak.
  • Low if anything is Weak, or eligibility is still uncertain.

Don't give yourself a High while any eligibility criterion is unconfirmed. If I had to keep only one rule from this check, it would be that one. Ignoring it is how organisations spend weeks on proposals that get screened out before a reviewer reads them.

What each verdict asks of you

A High is not a reason to relax. List the three RFP requirements most likely to be thin in your first draft. Skip the obvious ones you'll cover anyway and look for the ones that sink proposals that looked fundable: evaluation plans, sustainability after the grant ends, letters of support you haven't asked for yet.

A Medium means listing two or three concrete actions that would move a Partial to a Strong before the deadline, such as a partner letter, outcome data you have but haven't pulled, or a conversation with the programme officer. If you can't name actions that fit the timeline, treat it as a Low.

With a Low, write the blocker down in one plain sentence and move on. "The funder requires three years of direct housing services; we have none." Once it's written that plainly, it's much easier to let go.

A hypothetical example

A workforce development organisation in Denver, seven years old with a modest budget, finds a housing stability fund for the metro area.

DimensionScoreWhy
Mission alignmentPartialClients face housing risk, but the mission is employment
EligibilityStrongGeography, budget and years operating all match
CapacityPartialOne pilot financial coaching workshop; funder expects housing programming

The verdict is Medium. Before applying, they'd want a letter of support from a housing partner and client data showing housing outcomes. If they can get both before the deadline, it's worth writing. If not, it isn't.

What the check can't tell you

It won't give you a probability of winning, and I don't believe anyone can give you a percentage that means anything. It doesn't replace knowing the funder, either. If you've spoken to the programme officer, that conversation counts for more than any score here.
