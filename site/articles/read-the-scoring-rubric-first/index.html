Read the scoring rubric first

Most people read an RFP from the top. Background, funder history, priorities, eligibility, and somewhere near the end, sometimes in an appendix, a table of how proposals will be scored. I'd start with that table. It's the one part of the document that says how reviewers are meant to judge your proposal, so it should decide the shape of your outline before you write a sentence.

Find the scoring section

It goes by several names: evaluation criteria, review criteria, selection criteria, scoring rubric, merit review. Search the document for "points" and "criteria", and check the attachments and FAQ too.

If there's no scoring section at all, you have less to go on, but not nothing. Use the funder's stated priorities, any list of what a strong application includes, and the order of the required sections. Then ask the programme officer how applications are reviewed. It's an ordinary question.

Copy the rubric into a table

Give every criterion its own row, with its points and the funder's exact wording. Don't summarise. The wording often hides sub-criteria. "Describes the need using local data and explains why current services are insufficient" is two things a reviewer can check off, and a summary like "need statement" loses the second one.

Then add two columns of your own: where the RFP wants this content to go, and what you already have that answers it. Leave that second column blank when you have nothing. The blanks are the point of the exercise.

Turn the points into a page budget

Once the points are in front of you, divide your space roughly in proportion to them. Here's a hypothetical RFP with a 10-page narrative limit and 100 points:

CriterionPointsShare of 10 pagesWhat we have
Need and population served202 pagesCounty data, our own intake numbers
Programme design303 pagesLogic model, staffing plan
Evaluation plan252.5 pagesOutcome measures, no evaluator yet
Organisational capacity151.5 pagesTwo past grants of a similar size
Budget and sustainability101 pageBudget drafted, sustainability plan thin

Two things stand out in a table like this. The evaluation plan is worth a quarter of the score, and it's an easy section to leave until the last evening. Meanwhile the organisational history, the part that tends to sprawl because it's the easiest to write, is worth 15 points.

Proportional isn't a law. If the RFP sets its own page limits per section, follow those. Also check whether it lists items that must be present for the application to be reviewed at all, such as eligibility documents or a budget form. Those may carry no points, but missing one can get you screened out. Still, proportional beats the usual default, which is writing each section until it feels done.

Use the funder's headings in the funder's order

Someone scoring against a rubric wants to find each criterion quickly. Give them the funder's headings, in the order the rubric lists them, and use the funder's words in the first sentence of each section. If a criterion has three sub-parts, answer them in that order.

This can feel mechanical. It is mechanical, and that's fine. A reviewer working through a score sheet isn't awarding points for elegant transitions. If the RFP prescribes a different section order, use that, and keep every criterion easy to find.

Spend your time where big points meet big gaps

Go back to the "what we have" column. A gap in a 5-point criterion costs you a short paragraph. A gap in a 25-point criterion is this week's work: pull the outcome data, ask the partner for a letter, call an evaluator about a fee.

If the large gaps can't be closed by the deadline, better to know now. It may mean this opportunity isn't a fit.

Score your own draft

When the draft is done, give it and the rubric to a colleague who didn't write it. Ask them to score it criterion by criterion, the way a reviewer would, and to tell you where they had to hunt for an answer. Anything they couldn't find counts as missing. To a reviewer, it is.
