Why a topic isn't enough to start writing

A topic like "AI in the workplace" or "why I changed my mind about X" feels like enough to start an issue. Sit down with only that and a blank doc, and you can lose an hour.

What's missing is a plan: exactly what you're claiming, why it's worth a reader's time, and how the issue gets from the hook to the call to action without losing them.

Skip that step and issues tend to meander. The skill does the planning and leaves the writing to you.

What the skill does

  1. Paste your topic or idea It can be rough. "I want to write about how most creators undercharge for sponsorships" is enough.
  2. It turns the topic into a claim A specific point someone could agree or disagree with, shown next to your original topic so you can see what changed.
  3. Then it plans the rest Three headline options (direct, curiosity and contrarian), three sections with word counts, one call to action and three subject lines.
Example output from the skill for a sample request
The request

Plan my next issue. Topic: most small newsletters price sponsorships badly. My newsletter is for people who run newsletters.

Assumption

Your readers are solo or small-team newsletter operators who sell, or are about to sell, their own sponsorships. You gave the audience as "people who run newsletters"; I narrowed it to those who handle sponsorships themselves.

Sharpened topic

Starting point: "Most small newsletters price sponsorships badly."

Sharpened: Small newsletters price sponsorships badly because they borrow the per-thousand (CPM) maths built for big lists, when they should charge a flat fee based on who their readers are.

This is my proposed reading of "badly", not something you told me. If you meant something else (too low, no rate card, discounting too quickly), tell me and I'll re-plan around that.

Hook/headline options

Direct: Small newsletters shouldn't price sponsorships by CPM

Curiosity: The pricing habit that makes a small newsletter look cheaper than it is

Contrarian: Your list isn't too small to charge properly. Your pricing formula is the problem.

Issue structure
SectionDescriptionWords
1. The borrowed formulaHow small operators usually arrive at a price, and why list size multiplied by a going rate undersells a small list. Anchor it with [a real rate or pricing example you have seen or used].150
2. What a sponsor is actually buyingThe case for pricing on audience fit and the sponsor's outcome instead of raw reach. Support it with [your evidence: a sponsor conversation, a result, or a comparison you can stand behind].350
3. Re-pricing your next slotA short, practical way for the reader to set a flat fee and present it to a sponsor, using [the steps or rule of thumb you actually recommend].200

Total: 700 words.

CTA

Reply with your take: how did you arrive at your current sponsorship price?

I don't know your subscriber count, so this is the default. If you have 500 or more, tell me and I'll swap in a purchase, hire or referral ask.

Subject lines

Direct: Why CPM pricing fails small newsletters

Curiosity gap: You priced your sponsorship slot by list size, and...

Contrarian: A small list is not a reason to charge less

Before you write

The bracketed placeholders need your own material. I have not supplied any rates, results or statistics, because you didn't give me any.

What it does with vague or incomplete input

If the topic is too broad, it says so and offers three narrower versions before planning anything.

Coming back to an old topic with a new angle is fine. It plans around the angle.

If you leave out who your audience is, it states an assumption and carries on rather than stalling over one empty field.
